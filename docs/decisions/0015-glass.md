# 0015 — Glass

- **Status:** Agreed, 2026-11-12. Shared backdrop sample, blur before refraction and opaque regions added 2026-11-14.
- **Decision:**
  - The library's own `GlassBorder` in the Style tier ([0004](0004-tier-names.md)): a Skia backdrop blur of what is behind it in the window, plus a tint and a rim (below). Its content is clipped to the shape.
  - It refracts the backdrop like a thick glass slab. Content behind is displaced (lensed) near the rim within an edge band and only blurred in the flat centre. The order is blur, then refract, then tint, then the rim.
    - Why blur first (amended 2026-11-14 from refract, then blur): one shared blur per frame for all glass (see Shared backdrop sample) is only possible in this order. The difference is confined to the refraction band: rendered side by side over the test backdrop, the flat centre is within 2 levels (mean 0.7) and the band within 17 levels (mean 2.7), largest where a sharp feature crosses the edge; minimal glass, which has no refraction, is within 3 levels.
  - The blur and refraction run on a half-resolution copy of the backdrop (relative to the screen scaling), then scale back up, so they are cheaper (about half the cost). A pixel test checks the look against full resolution.
  - Shared backdrop sample:
    - `StandardsWindow` takes one sample of its backdrop per frame, right after the `Backdrop` slot is drawn: a copy at half resolution, blurred once. Its first-level glass (the content panel, and any first-level glass in the window's layers) reads that one blurred sample through its own refraction, dispersion and tint, with no backdrop blur of its own.
    - The sample is kept between frames, in two textures per window. A frame copies and blurs again only its redrawn region, and only where glass reads it: the glass less the opaque regions of its content (below), widened by the refraction's reach. Inside the redrawn region the glass matches a full redraw, except that sample texels the region's edge cuts through (one texel, 2 px at the half-resolution scale) keep their last value.
    - When the glass reading the sample changes (a surface appears or leaves, moves or resizes, or the opaque regions of its content change, for example while an opaque card scrolls partly out of view), the whole frame is redrawn that frame. The textures are kept: they are allocated again, empty, only when the window's size or screen scaling changes or the GPU device is lost, and freed when no glass reads the sample.
    - Overlays (menus, flyouts, notifications, dialogs), the full screen reveal sheet and second-level glass keep their own backdrop blur, clipped to their visible part: what is behind them includes content drawn after the sample. Glass in a window that is not a `StandardsWindow` blurs its backdrop itself, as before.
    - Glass with a blur other than the theme's (`GlassBlurSigma`) blurs its backdrop itself.
    - It needs a surface to read. Without one, and after the textures are allocated again until a frame has filled them in full, the glass blurs its backdrop itself.
    - Why: each fill's own backdrop blur is a separate Skia layer with its own copy and filter passes, about 0.7 ms of GPU time each (see Performance); one sample replaces them with one blur and one draw per surface.
  - Opaque regions:
    - Content marks itself opaque once, with the attached property `OpaqueRegion.IsOpaque` ([0017](0017-app-usage.md)). The region follows it as it moves, resizes, scrolls, is clipped, hidden or faded: it is recomputed after every layout pass and, before the frame is drawn, after a change of opacity, transform, clip or effect on it or an ancestor, including a transform or clip geometry changed in place (an animated `TranslateTransform.X`, a `RectangleGeometry.Rect`).
    - A marked control counts while it is effectively visible, it and its ancestors are at full opacity with no opacity mask or effect, and it stays axis-aligned. Its rounded corners are left out (twice the radius for squircle shapes), and its ancestors' clips cut it.
    - Glass whose fill is the solid fallback with an opaque tint counts as opaque too, so with `GlassQuality` `Solid` or reduced transparency the content panel is cut out of the backdrop.
    - A glass fill skips the opaque regions of its own content. `StandardsWindow` clips its `Backdrop` to the rest of the window and exposes that region as `BackdropVisibleRegion` ([0022](0022-window-layout.md)).
    - The opaque areas are shrunk by the glass read reach before they are cut out of the backdrop: three blur sigmas plus the furthest refraction displacement (18 + 28 × 1.35, about 56 px with the theme's values): how far glass beside an opaque area reads the backdrop under it. With no glass drawn as glass the reach is 0.
    - Why: with an opaque document covering about 65% of a full-size window (Apple A18 Pro, 60 Hz, 2x scale, animated backdrop), full glass cost about 10 ms per frame more than Solid while blurring under it. Measured headless on a prototype of the shared sample (CPU Skia, 1280×800 at 2x, minimal glass, animated backdrop), skipping that document cut the glass's cost over Solid from about 25 ms to about 8 ms per frame. In a real window on the same hardware (one launch, 1344×787 at 2x, 60 Hz, animated backdrop, opaque document over about 65% of the window), every path, Solid included, held about 59–60 fps with the backdrop animating at 60 fps, at 5.5–6.3 ms of CPU per frame and a 95th-percentile frame interval of 17.7–19.6 ms, so the 10 ms gap between full glass and Solid is not measurable from the frame rate here. With the backdrop animating at 30 fps, in both passes, the shared sample with the opaque cut-out took about 11.7–11.9 ms of CPU per frame against 9.9–10.6 ms for glass blurring its own backdrop, with the 95th-percentile frame interval at 51–53 ms on both. A second pass at 60 fps fell to about 39–44 fps on every path, Solid included, which is a change in the system rather than in the glass, so it is not used. The process's own GPU time per frame could not be recorded, so the shared sample's GPU saving in a real window has not been measured.
  - Rim:
    - Every glass surface gets a hairline rim right on the edge, with a soft glow falling inward from it.
    - Its width follows the local brightness: a hairline over dim areas, swelling smoothly, inward only, where the backdrop under that part of the edge is bright.
    - It takes a light tint of what is directly outside each point of the edge (the lightened local backdrop colour, hue kept), and its intensity follows the local brightness, so it is faint over dark areas and never uniform.
    - Corners and the top edge catch slightly more light.
    - Where a bright feature, such as a thin line, touches the edge, the rim takes on its tint and brightness at that point, and the light runs along the edge in both directions before fading. The crossing blends into that run with no odd pixel.
    - The rim replaces the fixed 1 px border on glass. The fixed border colours (see Values) stay only for the solid fallback (see Fallbacks), which is otherwise unchanged.
    - Rim values:
      - Width 1 px, swelling to 1.75 px over the brightest backdrop.
      - Inner glow 4 px beyond the rim's width, starting at half the rim's strength and fading to nothing.
      - Sampled 1.5 px outside the edge.
      - Lightened towards white by 0.3 in light and 0.6 in dark.
      - Top edge bias 1.0, sides and bottom 0.7.
      - Strength 1: the rim's opacity follows the local brightness at full scale.
      - Carried light runs 12 px along the edge at full strength in each direction, then fades over about 8 px. Its excess over the surrounding brightness counts 1.7 times.
      - White mix 0.5 in both themes: catches are half the feature's colour, half white.
      - Bright stretches and catches are amplified by up to 100% (boost 1), ramping smoothly with the backdrop's luminance from 0.4 to 0.9. The same ramp widens the rim inward by up to 0.25 px, within the 1.75 px maximum, so the boost shows even where the rim is already near white.
    - Why these values: they were tuned live in the catalog against its ribbon backdrop. The gain was lowered to 1.7 and the run along the edge doubled to 12 px, for a longer, softer run of light. The 4 px inner glow makes the edge read as lit glass rather than a drawn line. A white mix of 0.5 in both themes keeps catches partly the feature's colour and partly white, and a boost of 1 doubles the bright catches so they stand out.
    - How it is drawn:
      - By default the rim reads the backdrop along the edge into a strip, one texel per device pixel of the perimeter (rows of up to 4096 texels, a texture size every GPU supports). Three small passes along the strip work out the carried light (the brightest colour within 12 px, faded, against the darkest). One draw over the band then shades each pixel from the squircle's exact distance (an analytic distance in a shader, within about 0.05 px of the outline).
      - It skips the work outside the region Avalonia redraws, and it takes the current opacity. It reuses the strip when that region holds none of the edge's sample points. When it holds some, only those are read again: outside the region the surface holds the last frame, with whatever was drawn above the glass.
      - Limits: the region is one rectangle, since `.UseUiStandards()` keeps Avalonia's one dirty rectangle per frame ([0017](0017-app-usage.md)). Every point is read when the rim's visual renders again (a change of size, shape, theme or rim method), since each render creates a new draw operation. Opacity changes and fades do not render it again.
      - Inside an effect or opacity-mask layer (with `SkiaOptions.UseOpacitySaveLayer`, also below full opacity), without a surface to read, or when the strip's textures cannot be allocated, the rim uses Skia backdrop filters instead (a displacement, a dilate, a blur and an erode per strip along the edge), with the same distance shader for its geometry. The surface does not hold a layer's content until the layer ends, so the strip would read the wrong backdrop there.
      - The two methods differ by under 1.5 levels on average over the rim's pixels. The strip carries light along the edge itself and the filters across a square, so a few catches within 20 px of a corner differ more.
      - Why: the strip draws the rim in about a tenth of the GPU time of the filters, with about a twentieth of their CPU time. Its geometry needs no rasterised masks, so a new size (a resize or a sidebar collapse) costs no rebuild.
  - Values:
    - Blur sigma 6 ("light frost").
    - Refraction strength 28 px over a 28 px edge band.
    - Chromatic dispersion on, at a subtle 0.35.
    - Tint and border:

      | | Light | Dark |
      |---|---|---|
      | Tint colour | `#FFFFFF` | `#1E1E1E` |
      | Tint opacity | 0.68 | 0.68 |
      | 1 px border | `#99FFFFFF` (white 60%) | `#33FFFFFF` (white 20%) |
  - Theme keys: `GlassTintColor`, `GlassTintOpacity`, `GlassBorderBrush`, `GlassBlurSigma`, `GlassRefractionStrength`, `GlassRefractionEdge`, `GlassChromaticDispersion`.
  - It is used on overlays (menus, flyouts, tooltips, dialogs), on the window's content panel ([0022](0022-window-layout.md)), and on cards only where they sit directly on the window backdrop. Cards inside the content panel are solid (card colour). Sidebars are not glass.
    - Tooltips are solid; see [0037](0037-tooltip-and-notifications.md).
    - Glass overlays open in the window's overlay layer, so they stay inside the window; see [0036](0036-menus-and-flyouts.md).
  - Glass nests two levels deep. A glass surface inside one glass surface (the second level) is minimal glass; a third level and deeper is the solid fill. Solid ancestors do not count as levels.
  - Quality:
    - Full glass: refraction, dispersion, blur, tint and the rim.
    - Minimal glass: blur, tint and the rim, with no refraction or dispersion.
    - With `GlassQuality` `Full` (the default; [0017](0017-app-usage.md)), the first level is full glass. The second level, and any glass whose shorter side is under 64 px, is minimal glass. `Minimal` makes every glass level minimal; `Solid` makes every glass surface the solid fill. `GlassQuality` is the app's performance switch, separate from the reduce-transparency accessibility setting, which makes glass solid whatever the quality.
    - The quality does not change under load.
    - Why: refraction is the costliest part of the fill and reads only on the outer level. Under 64 px the 28 px refraction bands leave under 8 px of flat centre, and full and minimal glass render the same there. A fixed quality keeps every frame looking the same.
  - GPU budget: `.UseUiStandards()` sets Skia's GPU resource budget to 256 MB ([0017](0017-app-usage.md)). Why: glass needs about 190 MB of GPU scratch in a full-screen window with several surfaces. Under Avalonia's default budget (about 29 MB), Skia frees and reallocates its textures every frame.
  - Performance:
    - Measured on an Apple A18 Pro (Metal) at 2x, over the catalog's animated ribbons at the display rate (60 Hz). The ribbons alone run at 58 fps with a 9.4 ms render frame.
    - The rim takes 0.5 to 1.1 ms of CPU and GPU time per frame for a full-window panel with four cards; the filter rim takes 7 to 20 ms. The fill (blur, tint and refraction) is the larger cost.
    - Full glass side by side on the backdrop holds 30 fps up to about 16 surfaces in a normal window and 24 surfaces full screen. 30 fps is first lost at 24 and 32 surfaces respectively.
    - Minimal glass at the second level, side by side in one panel, holds 30 fps up to about 33 surfaces. It is lost by 65.
    - Where 30 fps is lost, the GPU is the limit: about 20 ms of GPU time per frame against 8 to 10 ms of CPU, mostly the fills' backdrop blurs (about 0.7 ms each). These figures are for each fill blurring its own backdrop, before the shared sample.
    - A glass fill cannot know when the backdrop changes just outside the region Avalonia redraws: glass pixels within the blur's reach of a change but outside the redrawn region keep the last frame until they are redrawn. This holds for the shared sample and the own blur alike.
  - Fallbacks:
    - Without Skia runtime effects, the blur stays and refraction and dispersion drop.
    - Without Skia, when "reduce transparency" is on, or with `GlassQuality` `Solid`, it is a solid fill in the neutral palette ([0011](0011-colour.md)).
    - Glass in a popup shown as its own OS window is the solid fill; see [0036](0036-menus-and-flyouts.md).
  - The library reads "reduce transparency" from the OS once, when the theme is created (macOS accessibility settings, Windows transparency setting). Linux has no such setting, so it stays "not reduced" unless the app forces it. Apps can force it through `StandardsTheme` ([0017](0017-app-usage.md)).
  - Web ([0045](0045-web-library.md)):
    - The class `ui-glass` draws blur (sigma 6), tint and rim on pseudo-elements, so the element itself is not a backdrop root and nested glass blurs the page as on desktop. The levels, the solid fill and its border are the same; `data-glass-quality` and `setGlassQuality()` set the quality. Levels count within one layer: an element in the top layer (a popover or a modal `<dialog>`) starts a new count wherever it sits in the page, as desktop overlays open in the window's overlay layer ([0036](0036-menus-and-flyouts.md)), so menus, submenus, flyouts and notifications are first-level glass.
    - Refraction and dispersion are on by default in Chromium, where `ui.js` gives each first-level glass an SVG displacement filter in `backdrop-filter`, sized to the element. Safari and Firefox do not apply SVG filters in `backdrop-filter`, so they get minimal glass. The under-64 px rule also needs the script. Measured in Chrome at 2x over a moving backdrop: 16 full-glass cards 48 fps and one 1100×600 panel 42 fps, against 60 for minimal glass.
    - The rim is a fixed hairline (top 1.0, sides 0.7 as `--ui-glass-rim-side-bias`, a 4 px inner glow; `--ui-glass-rim-color` white at 0.7 light and 0.067 dark, fitted to the content panel over the backdrop wash). Over a bright backdrop the dark rim is therefore much fainter than desktop's. CSS cannot sample the backdrop along the edge, so it does not swell or tint with the local backdrop, carry light along the edge, or boost bright stretches.
    - Reduce transparency follows `prefers-reduced-transparency` where the browser supports it (Chromium); in Safari and Firefox it stays "not reduced" unless the app sets it with `setReduceTransparency`.
- **Why:** The glass is the same on every OS and in headless renders. Avalonia's `ExperimentalAcrylicBorder` renders nothing headless, and OS window effects differ per platform. Refraction makes the surface read as real glass, not a tinted overlay.
